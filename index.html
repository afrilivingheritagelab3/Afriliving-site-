Our mission

To bridge the gap between aging elders holding indigenous wisdom and tech-savvy youth by facilitating ethical, community-governed fieldwork, measuring real knowledge transfer, and deploying digital tools to keep African intangible cultural heritage vibrant and alive.

Our vision

To be Africa's leading youth-driven movement for living heritage preservation, transforming cultural conservation from passive digital archiving into active, intergenerational knowledge transmission that empowers future generations.

    About us

    Heritage that is practised, not only stored

    AfriLiving Heritage Lab bridges the elders who hold our living history and the young people who want to connect with their roots.

    Our logo

    The face of the Lab

    Who we are

    Youth stepping up

    We are a youth-led initiative, run by young people. We began in Kenya with a Pan-African vision, we work with youth across Kenya and Africa, and we are growing into a worldwide movement.

    Young people are too often treated as an audience or as trainees in cultural work. Here they lead the research, look after the data and curate the stories, in partnership with their communities.

    Leadership is shared and handed on. As each generation of organisers moves on, new young leaders step in, so the Lab always belongs to the youth who carry it.

    Founder

    Dimina Asila Khasoha

    AfriLiving Heritage Lab was founded by Dimina Asila Khasoha, Founder and Lead Coordinator, who started the Lab in Kenya so that young people could lead the work of safeguarding living traditions in the digital age.

    The Lab is built to outlast its founder. Leadership is being shared with, and handed on to, new young leaders from across Kenya and Africa.

    Connect with the founder on LinkedIn

    Why we exist

    Living heritage is at risk

    Three in four Kenyans are under 35 (2019 Census, KNBS). Much living heritage, such as oral traditions, songs, proverbs and ecological knowledge, is held by elders. Many young people grow up far from them.

    When an elder passes away without passing on what they know, part of an unwritten library disappears. Recording a voice is not enough. A tradition no one practises is not heritage, it is a memory.

    • Generations drift apart. Migration, urban life and schooling often separate young people from the elders who hold the knowledge.
    • Knowledge is unwritten. Songs, stories, remedies and skills live in people, so they can be lost when the person is gone.
    • Attention moves online. Global feeds compete with local languages and practices for young people's time.
    • Archives are not enough. A recording stored away does not mean anyone has learned it or will practise it.

    Read the blog behind this idea on LinkedIn

    The gap we close

    From recording to passing on

    Much heritage work is led by institutions and centres on collecting and storing. Young people are rarely asked what they think, and few projects report whether anything was actually passed on.

    We work the other way round. Young people lead, communities decide what is shared, and we measure whether knowledge moved from one generation to the next.

    Global goals

    How this connects to the SDGs

    Our work contributes to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. We describe the link, we do not claim results we have not yet measured.

    SDG 4.7

    Education for culture

    Learning that values cultural diversity and the contribution of culture to sustainable development, through elders teaching young people.

    SDG 11.4

    Safeguarding heritage

    Strengthening efforts to protect cultural heritage by keeping it practised and shared, not only stored.

    SDG 10

    Reduced inequalities

    Giving young people and elders a voice, and letting communities control how their knowledge is used.

    SDG 17

    Partnerships

    Working with communities, schools, cultural institutions and youth groups across Kenya, Africa and beyond.

    How we work

    Three parts, one aim

    Listening first

    Youth Voices dialogues ask young people how they relate to their culture, what disconnects them and what would inspire them to take part.

    Active intergenerational transfer

    Through Sauti Zetu, young people sit down with elders in their own families to ask, record and learn traditions they did not know before.

    Ethical curation and protection

    Consent is a conversation, not a signature. Sacred knowledge stays protected, and approved stories become modern formats.

    How we measure

    The Transmission Delta

    We measure success by the gap closed between what a young person knew before and what an elder successfully taught them. We also track who practises, and who teaches someone else.

    A three-month pilot shows early signs, not final proof. We will say so in our report.

    Our commitments

    Community decides

    • Free, prior and informed consent before anything is recorded, with the right to withdraw at any time.
    • Tiered access: public, community-only or restricted, decided by the community.
    • Written benefit-sharing agreements, and no commercial use of restricted material.
    • Minors only with school and guardian permission.
    • We are guided by the UNESCO 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, in particular Articles 13, 14 and 15.

    Programmes

    What we run

    20 November 2026

    Youth Voices

    Interviews and open dialogues with young people. We ask what intangible cultural heritage means to them, how connected they feel to their traditions, whether traditional practices still matter today, how technology shapes their relationship with culture, what makes young people interested or uninterested, and what role they should play in safeguarding it. We also ask which one tradition, story, song or form of knowledge they would preserve for future generations. Young people can answer in the way that suits them: a short essay, a video, a piece of art or a photo. Results shape everything that follows.

    Late Nov to mid Dec 2026

    Sauti Zetu

    Sauti Zetu means "our voices". Young people are paired with elders and knowledge-holders to document oral histories and living traditions, and can share what they learn as an essay, a video, artwork or a photo. It is open across Kenya. Only the launch takes place on campus.

    Early December 2026

    Beyond Borders

    A one-day virtual symposium that brings together young people from Kenya, across Africa, the Caribbean and beyond to explore tangible and intangible heritage, AI and digital technology. Registration is open.

    January 2027

    Resilient Whispers

    The climate and heritage connection: traditional ecological knowledge, climate adaptation and community resilience, told by young people with their elders.

    Schools and communities come later, and only after the right permissions and consent are in place.

    Before you apply

    What to know

    • The pilot is for adults who consent. Anyone under 18 can take part only with school and guardian permission.
    • We use your details only for the Lab, and you can ask us to remove them.
    • Your answers are seen only by the Lab team.
    • We collect your name, contact details and answers. We use them only to run the Lab and do not sell them or share them for advertising. To ask us to remove them, message the Lab on Instagram or use the Contact page.
    • Joining is voluntary. No one is pressured to share anything they would rather keep private.
